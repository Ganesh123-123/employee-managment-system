# DECISIONS.md

1. **Indexes.** On `employees`: unique index on `emp_code` (guarantees unique identity across concurrent creations) and compound index on `("department", 1), ("joined_on", 1)` (powers headcount filtering in analytics). On `attendance_logs`: unique compound on `("emp_code", 1), ("date", 1)` (enforces one record per employee-day and blocks duplicate punch-in races); `("date", -1), ("emp_code", 1)` (serves attendance list sorting/pagination in MongoDB); `("emp_code", 1), ("punch_in", -1)` (backs punch-out lookup across midnight); and `("date", 1), ("late_minutes", 1)` (serves late leaderboard queries). Considered and rejected: index on `status` alone (low cardinality, minimal selectivity compared to date-based bounds).

2. **Punch-in race.** Both requests validate payloads and attempt atomic insertion into `attendance_logs`. Backed by the unique compound index on `(emp_code, date)`, MongoDB atomically permits exactly one insertion to succeed, returning 201. The second concurrent operation violates the constraint, triggering PyMongo's `DuplicateKeyError`, which our exception handler catches and translates immediately into HTTP 409 Conflict without any check-then-insert window.

3. **Ties.** The leaderboard pipeline utilizes `$setWindowFields` with `$rank` to calculate standard competition ranking (1, 2, 2, 4). The `limit` filter (`rank <= limit`) checks the computed rank rather than array index. Tied employees at the cutoff are both included (the response may exceed `limit` rows), ordered deterministically by `total_late_minutes` descending, then `emp_code` ascending.

4. **Headcount.** The department summary aggregation starts from the `employees` collection filtering `joined_on <= last_day_of_month`. Each matching employee contributes 1 to departmental headcount. Attendance records are joined via `$lookup`. Employees with zero records yield an empty logs array, preserving their headcount contribution while contributing zero to attendance counts.

5. **One thing you would change for 100x data.** At tens of millions of records, querying across wide date ranges becomes I/O heavy. I would implement an event-driven incremental rollup pattern that updates pre-aggregated monthly summary documents upon punch-out and regularization, allowing monthly analytics to serve O(1) point lookups instead of multi-stage collection aggregations.
